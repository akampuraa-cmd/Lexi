The history of artificial intelligence dates back to the mid-20th century, when pioneers like Alan Turing and John McCarthy laid the groundwork for machines that could simulate human reasoning.
Turing's famous 1950 paper asked the question, "Can machines think?" and proposed what is now known as the Turing Test, a measure of a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from that of a human.

The field grew slowly at first, hampered by the limited computational power of early computers and the optimistic but ultimately difficult problems researchers chose to tackle.
During the 1980s, expert systems became popular, encoding human knowledge into rule-based programs that could reason about specific domains.
However, these systems were brittle and required massive manual effort to maintain.

The rise of machine learning in the 1990s and 2000s shifted the paradigm.
Instead of encoding rules by hand, researchers began building systems that could learn patterns from data.
Neural networks, inspired loosely by the structure of the human brain, became increasingly powerful tools.

Deep learning — the use of many-layered neural networks trained on large datasets — revolutionized AI starting around 2012, when convolutional networks achieved dramatic improvements in image recognition.
This success inspired researchers to apply similar architectures to natural language, culminating in transformer-based models like GPT and BERT.

Today, large language models can write code, compose essays, answer questions, and engage in conversations with remarkable fluency.
Yet despite their power, these models lack true understanding, consciousness, or common sense in the way humans experience the world.

The future of AI remains open: researchers continue to explore reasoning, planning, memory, and alignment — ensuring that increasingly capable systems remain safe and beneficial for humanity.
Language is the fabric of human thought. Through words, we share ideas, build cultures, and pass knowledge across generations.
A model that truly understands language would need to grasp not just words, but context, intent, emotion, and the vast background of human experience.

Training such a model from scratch is a journey into the very structure of intelligence.
Every token the model learns to predict is a small step toward capturing the patterns that make language meaningful.
With enough data, computation, and careful design, even a small model can begin to reflect the richness of human expression.
